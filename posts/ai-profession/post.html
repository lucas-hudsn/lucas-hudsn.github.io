Maybe AI needs a regulated profession behind it

My opinion after 3 years in the APS — and three stories from this month

In my opinion, almost anyone can deploy AI that makes big decisions about people with no profession in the middle. Three recent Australian stories show why I think we need registered AI engineers.

NotePersonal view

This is just my personal opinion as a relatively novice Data Scientist. This is not my employer’s view — just half-formed thoughts I’ve been turning over.

In my opinion, we have a gap: almost anyone with the right tech skills can put an AI model live that makes big decisions about real people, and almost nothing stands in the way.

A new grad can do it within weeks of starting. So can a contractor, a vendor selling into government, or a startup founder. I’m not picking on grads — they’re just the clearest example I’ve seen. My point is broader: there is no profession in the middle checking this work. So power sits with whoever has the computers and the confidence to ship, not with anyone accountable for getting it right.

I’m not a policymaker or a lawyer, so I’m probably missing a lot here. But as I understand it, Australia’s approach is still up in the air. We had a Voluntary AI Safety Standard in 2024, then mandatory guardrails were floated that same year, then the National AI Plan in December 2025 stepped back and said “just use existing law.” Then in July 2026 the Prime Minister changed direction again and announced mandatory “Australian Standards for AI” plus a new Office of AI, with a law possibly in early 2027.

So there’s a live conversation happening right now, and lots of smarter people than me are debating what to do. I’m not saying this post has the answer. I just wanted to put forward one possible way to look at it — what if part of the answer was about people and professions, not just rules and guardrails?

Three stories from this month helped shape my thinking here, and in my view they show why this angle might be worth talking about.

1. Fake AI citations were used to silence someone on parole

As reported by The Guardian on 26 Sep 2026, Tasmania’s Parole Board banned Susan Neill-Fraser — convicted of murder in 2010, a conviction she has always disputed — from talking to the media to claim she is innocent.

The problem: the document used to justify that ban cited court cases that do not exist. They were AI hallucinations.

To me, this is exactly the risk I worry about. A low-transparency decision — parole conditions that are very rarely challenged in court — plus unchecked use of a general AI tool by a public servant, and the result is someone’s basic freedom to speak is taken away based on made-up law.

This is why, in my opinion, my dividing line matters. If a lawyer uses ChatGPT badly for one document, that’s already covered by legal rules. But when government staff use AI to justify decisions about people’s liberty, I think that should need a professional behind it — someone trained, registered, and able to lose their registration if they are negligent.

2. An OpenAI agent broke into Medicare — and we found out months later

As reported by The Guardian on 24 Sep 2026, an OpenAI agent given a harmless research task — compiling health stats — got into non-public files in Medicare’s statistics portal, plus the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare, Victoria’s Department of Health, and the NSW Bureau of Crime Statistics.

No personal medical records are believed to have been taken. But OpenAI only told Australia on 10 September, about three months after the June incident, via an email to a generic government inbox that gets checked once a day.

In my view, this shows two things:

  1. A handful of huge labs can ship autonomous agents without good safeguards, and we all wear the risk. That is power pooling in too few hands.

  2. Post-market rules are missing. If a person had hacked Medicare like this, we’d call it hacking. Because it was an agent, our disclosure laws look out of date, as experts in that story said. Prof Toby Walsh is quoted saying we should prosecute — I’m less settled on criminal law, but I agree we need clear product-level consequences: fines, forced fixes, maybe deployment bans, closer to how ASIC or APRA work.

It also shows why I think health systems should be in the narrow pre-deployment check group. For credit, health, criminal justice and critical infrastructure, waiting until after harm shows up doesn’t seem good enough.

3. “My feed, my way” — making algorithms opt-out

As reported by The Guardian on 8 Sep 2026, Australia plans to force social platforms to let over-16s turn off algorithm-driven feeds and just see people they follow, with fines over A$100m. There is also a digital duty of care to protect under-18s from porn, eating-disorder content, misogyny, crime-glorifying stunts, and serious bullying, enforced by the eSafety Commissioner.

I like this approach, in my opinion, because it targets the product, not the individual worker. That is the split I want: punish the bad product quickly, and only go after the engineer personally if there was real negligence.

The Prime Minister framed it as “taking power from big global tech companies and giving it to ordinary Australians.” To me, a profession does the same thing in a different way — it stops the practical power to build these systems sitting only with whoever has the compute and confidence.

The gap, as I see it

If you want to say a building won’t fall down, you need to be a qualified structural engineer. If you want to put someone to sleep for surgery, you need to be a registered doctor.

But if you want to launch a model that decides who gets a loan, who gets flagged for fraud, or who gets help first in a queue? As far as I can tell, you don’t need to be anything in particular. No registration. No personal accountability. No body that can stop you practising if you get it badly wrong.

My rough idea: registered AI engineers

I don’t think we need to invent this from scratch. Australia already makes engineers register in several states. You can’t legally work in structural, civil or electrical without being on the register, and a board can suspend you.

My suggestion: create a new type — something like Deployed AI Systems Engineer — under that same sort of system. To sign off on a live model-based system, you’d need to be on the register.

What would you need to show? I’d guess:

  1. Ethics and bias testing. Not just a one-off training video. In my view, you’d need to show you can actually test for unfair or harmful outputs before launch.

  2. Using the right-sized tool. Knowing when you need a big language model and when a simple logistic regression — or no model at all — would do for way less cost and carbon. It’s small, but it’s one of the only places AI rules might touch emissions, and it might nudge people away from grabbing the biggest model every time.

  3. Risk checks before launch. A bit like the judgement a structural engineer uses before saying a building is safe to occupy.

On liability, my view is you should be judged against the standard that existed when you signed off — not a new standard invented after something went wrong. Otherwise no one would sign, and the whole idea would fail.

Who would run it? Just my guess

Engineer registration now is state-based. But AI isn’t — something built in Sydney is live in Perth the same afternoon. Bolting this onto six state schemes would, in my opinion, get messy.

My instinct is a single Commonwealth AI Engineering Register, set up through National Cabinet, would make more sense. National Cabinet is already meeting on AI standards as of August 2026, so the mechanism is arguably already there.

Enforcement — the bit I’m least sure about

Here’s where I’ve landed for now: I lean toward enforcement being mostly after launch, with pre-launch checks as a smaller layer just for the highest-risk systems.

Two levers, kept deliberately apart:

1. The product. If a live system’s outputs break published standards — racist lending, unsafe triage, fake legal bans like the Tasmania case — the system faces fines, forced fixes, maybe a deployment ban. One bad output shouldn’t end a career by itself.

2. The engineer. If the breach came from real negligence in how it was built or signed off, then it’s the engineer’s licence on the line — suspension or being struck off, like doctors or engineers face now.

I think keeping these separate matters. Otherwise it becomes “one mistake and you’re a criminal,” and no one would join. But we still need real teeth.

Pre-launch checks would sit on top, but I’d keep them narrow — in my opinion maybe just credit, health, criminal justice, critical infrastructure, like the Medicare case. Everything else could run on monitoring, audits and incident reports after launch. That’s faster and doesn’t block every low-risk model.

What I’d leave out

If someone just uses an off-the-shelf tool badly as an individual, existing law probably already covers it.

My rough rule: was this a custom or built-in AI system, offered to the public or making big decisions about people, built by someone acting as a professional? If yes, maybe it needs a registered engineer behind it. The Tasmania parole document and the Medicare agent, in my view, both pass that test. Just chatting with a chatbot badly does not.

Who should be covered? My instinct

I’d say government, big business and small business should all be in. Carving out small business would, in my view, just push risk down to whoever can handle it least. And government shouldn’t get an easier standard either — the Tasmania case shows why.

But the paperwork should scale with risk and size. A three-person startup tweaking an open model for internal use is not the same as a bank fraud system, and I think the rules should say that clearly.

One last thought

In my opinion Australia can’t just copy America here — light-touch rules and huge compute budgets have let a handful of labs set the terms for everyone else. If we built licensing and handed the keys to whoever’s biggest, we wouldn’t fix capture, we’d formalise it.

Whoever writes the course, sits on the board, and defines “meets the standard” shapes who gets to do AI here. So whatever body does this would need to be genuinely independent, its rule-making public, and the standard itself must explicitly protect open-model use rather than punish it for being checkable.

We already licence the people who say buildings are safe to stand in. I’m not sure why we don’t do something similar for people saying a model is safe to make decisions about people’s lives.

But I could easily be missing something, and I’d genuinely like to hear the counterargument.
